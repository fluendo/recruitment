# Question 4: Adding Bald-People Data Didn't Improve Accuracy

## 0. Rule out measurement noise first

Bald faces are a minority subgroup, so the bald slice of the evaluation
set is probably small. A metric computed over few samples has real
sampling variance (e.g., with 30 bald faces, a change from 20/30 to 22/30
recall looks like an improvement, but the binomial confidence interval on 
the total samples makes that the  two are not distinguishable from no 
change at all. The reverse holds too: an unchanged number is consistent 
with "no effect" but equally consistent with a real small improvement 
plus unlucky sampling.

**Check**: compute recall with a confidence interval on the bald subgroup
before and after. If the intervals overlap substantially, "no
improvement" is inconclusive, not a real negative result.
**Fix**: grow the bald-specific evaluation set (kept separate from the
training data) so the intervals get narrower than the effect size we care
about, then re-measure. 

Only treat the flat result as real, and move to the causes below, once the
intervals are narrow: the before and after intervals still overlap (no
change), and the bald subgroup is still clearly below the other groups.

## 1. The new data never reaches the model

Before looking at the model, check that the new images are really used in
training. A wrong path, a wrong class id, boxes in the wrong format, or a
filter (minimum size, augmentation crops) can silently drop them, and then
"nothing changed" is exactly what we would see.

**Check**: draw a few training batches with their boxes on top, and count how
many bald images show up per epoch.
**Fix**: correct the loading or the label format, then retrain.

## 2. Insufficient representation in the training mix

"Several" images added to a much larger dataset can be close to zero
gradient signal if there is no oversampling or loss reweighting toward
the new examples. This is probably the single most common practical
reason "we added data and nothing changed."

**Check**: look at training recall on just the bald subset (not
validation). If the model can't even fit its own bald training examples,
the problem is representation/optimization, not generalization.
**Fix**: oversample the bald examples, or reweight the loss toward them,
rather than adding more data the same way.

## 3. Box convention mismatch

If the new bald images were annotated with a different box convention
than the original set (e.g. how far above the eyebrows the box extends,
with no hairline to anchor to), IoU against ground truth is systematically
lower for those boxes even when the detector is finding the face
correctly. This alone can explain a flat IoU-based metric despite added
data, with no deeper model problem involved.

of 0.5) for the bald subgroup and for the other groups. A looser threshold

bald subgroup. That points at box geometry, not detection.

## 4. Hair as a learned shortcut

The model may be using hair/hairline as a cue for "this is a face". A
modest amount of added bald data may not be enough to unlearn a
correlation that held across the rest of the dataset.

**Check**: Take faces that originally have hair, remove the hair (image 
editing or generative inpainting), and compare detection scores with and 
without hair. As a control, apply an edit of similar size somewhere else 
on the image (for example a patch of background), so editing artifacts 
are not mistaken for the hair effect. A large score drop only when
the hair is removed points to the shortcut.
**Fix**: if confirmed, adding more bald images the same way probably
won't help, since that already failed. Break the correlation directly:
apply hair-removal augmentation to non-bald faces too, so hair stops being
predictive of "face" in general, in addition to (not instead of)
reweighting bald examples in the loss.
