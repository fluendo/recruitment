# Question 1 — Domain Shift Across Clubs, Camera Heights and Angles

## Most likely root causes

### 1. Viewpoint shift (camera height and angle)

Camera height and angle change the foreshortening (limbs pointing towards the
camera look shorter), the self-occlusion of the arm, the apparent size of the
player in pixels, and the position of the wrist in image coordinates. The pose
model sees images that look different from its training data, and the hit
classifier sees the same motion projected differently. A smash filmed from an
elevated camera looks very different from the same smash filmed at court level.

The hit classifier is the most exposed stage. If it uses absolute 2D positions,
projected arm angles or wrist height in the image, it is tied to the training
viewpoint.

### 2. Camera and stream parameters

- **Intrinsics**: field of view, lens distortion and resolution change player
  scale and keypoint geometry.
- **Frame rate**: velocity features and temporal windows are defined in frames,
  so a different fps changes their meaning.
- **Compression and motion blur**: different encoders and shutter settings
  degrade the wrist and hand keypoints first.

The camera position (the extrinsics) only matters directly if we use 3D or
metric features. For monocular 2D pose, it acts through the viewpoint effect
above.

### 3. Scene appearance

Illumination, court color, glass-wall reflections, artificial lighting flicker
and clothing differ between clubs. Modern pose backbones are usually robust, but
reflections and low-contrast backgrounds can still create false keypoints.

### 4. Player scale and occlusion

Far-court players are small and produce noisier keypoints. The net, the partner
and the walls occlude players differently in each club.

### 5. Validation that never measured generalization

If the validation split was made by frame or clip rather than by club or match,
the same courts, cameras and players appear in train and validation. Then the
accuracy only shows that the model memorized the training setups, and the drop
on new clubs is the real generalization gap becoming visible.

## Systematic investigation

### Step 1 — Rebuild the evaluation

- Re-split by club and camera setup so that no setup appears in both train and
  validation.
- Log metadata for every clip: camera height, angle, distance, field of view,
  resolution, fps, lighting condition, court type, player scale range.

### Step 2 — Separate the two stages

I'd test the two stages on their own, and then together:

- **Pose model**: PCK (Percentage of Correct Keypoints) and OKS (Object 
  Keypoint Similarity) per keypoint (wrists and elbows in particular), 
  to see if the keypoints get worse on the new setups.
- **Hit classifier**: per-class F1 and timing error, with ground-truth (GT)
  keypoints as input, to see if the classifier alone fails.
- **End to end**: per-class F1 and timing error on the full pipeline, with the
  predicted keypoints.

The first two tests need keypoint labels from the new clubs. If we don't have
them, I'd label a few hundred frames per club, or use a heavy model such as
ViTPose++ (Q2) as a rough reference.

If I give the classifier ground-truth keypoints, the pose model's mistakes are
out of the picture. Whichever stage gets worse is where the problem starts. If
both get worse, I compare the end-to-end drop with the two separate drops to see
how the errors add up.

### Step 3 — Stratify by scenario

I break the results down by camera height, angle, resolution, fps, lighting and
player scale, using the metadata from Step 1. This gives a first ranking of the
factors that go with the failures.

Clubs differ in many ways at once, so correlation alone is not enough. To
isolate a single factor:

- Apply synthetic changes to a fixed set of clips: viewpoint warp, brightness and
  contrast shift, downscaling, blur, compression, fps resampling.
- Where possible, group real clips in which only one factor differs.
- Check the synthetic results against the real per-club data, since synthetic
  changes do not fully reproduce real camera differences.

The output is a ranked list of factors by impact on each stage. It tells me
where to spend the fix effort.
