# Question 4 — Stable but Less Accurate vs. Accurate but Jittery

## Step 1 — Understand the difference between the runs

First I'd check whether the gap between the two runs is just normal run-to-run
variation. If it is, they are two draws from the same distribution and neither
is better by itself.

- **Measure the variance.** I train several seeds (for example 5) and compute
  the spread of each metric across seeds.
- **Check for actual problems.** With fixed seeds, a rerun should reproduce the
  result, up to tiny GPU differences. If it does not, something in the pipeline
  is uncontrolled: an unseeded split or augmentation, the data loading order, or
  non-deterministic GPU operations. I fix what I can before comparing anything.

## Step 2 — Metrics

Per-frame accuracy hides jitter and timing problems, so I combine
classification, keypoint and temporal metrics:

| Area | Metrics | What it tells us |

| Classification | Per-class precision, recall and F1, confusion matrix | Which hits fail, in particular fast ones (smashes, volleys) |
| Keypoint quality | PCK/OKS for wrists and elbows | Pose accuracy |
| Keypoint stability | Frame-to-frame acceleration and high-frequency energy of wrist and elbow trajectories | Jitter |
| Event level | Event precision and recall within a tolerance window, false events per minute of play, duplicated events | Whether the output is usable as hit events |
| Timing | Median and spread of the timing error | Consistency of the hit timing |
| Robustness | Change of the output under small input perturbations | Sensitivity of each model |

All metrics are reported with confidence intervals across seeds and across
matches.

## Step 3 — Interpreting the trade-off

- A stable model with lower accuracy can be **over-smoothed**: it drifts towards
  average poses and misses fast wrist motion. The per-class breakdown shows
  whether it fails on the fast hits.
- **Keypoint jitter can often be removed cheaply** with temporal smoothing (a
  One Euro or Kalman filter). So I compare both models with the same smoothing,
  the one that will be in the pipeline anyway. Smoothing can add lag and
  flatten fast wrist motion, so I tune it on smashes.
- **Timing inconsistency** in the hit detector is harder to fix with
  post-processing, so it weighs more against a model.

## Step 4 — Decision rule

I define the acceptable accuracy loss from the product requirements before
looking at the results, for example a maximum drop in smash recall and in
event-level recall. Then:

1. The accuracy gap is inside the seed-to-seed spread, or the stable model is
   within the accepted margin of the accurate one: choose the **stable model**.
2. The accuracy gap exceeds the margin, and smoothing resolves the jitter:
   choose the **accurate model with smoothing**.
3. The accuracy gap exceeds the margin, and the timing remains inconsistent
   after smoothing: choose the model with the better event-level metrics, and
   investigate the timing failures afterwards.

## Step 5 — Scenario-based evaluation

I evaluate both models on the same scenarios, with results broken down by
scenario:

- Different clubs, camera heights and angles (as in Q1).
- Partial occlusion and far-court players.
- Fast swings and each hit class, with smashes as a dedicated slice.
- Different frame rates and lighting conditions.
- Long continuous sequences, to expose drift and duplicated events.
- Shadow deployment on real match streams, where both models run in parallel
  without affecting users. There are no labels here, so I compare what can be
  measured without them: events per minute, duplicated events, jitter and how
  often the two models disagree. A person reviews a sample of the disagreements.

## Step 6 — Mixed and variance-reducing options

- **Combining components**: if the pose model and the hit classifier are
  separate, I can use the keypoints of the better pose model with the hit
  classifier of the other run. The classifier was trained on the keypoint
  distribution of its own pose model, so I validate the combination, or
  fine-tune the classifier on the new keypoints. It adds no computational cost.
- **EMA or checkpoint averaging** helps reduce run-to-run variance at no
  inference cost.
- **Ensemble distillation**: I distill an ensemble of several runs into one
  lightweight model, which keeps the inference cost low.
