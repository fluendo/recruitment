# Question 2 — Inconsistent Annotations (Keypoints and Hit Timestamps)

## Impact of inconsistent annotations

### On pose estimation

Wrists and elbows are small, fast-moving joints, and their exact position is
ambiguous. When annotators follow slightly different conventions, they add two kinds of error:

- **Random noise**: the model learns a blurred target and converges to an
  average location. This lowers precision and increases jitter.
- **Systematic bias**: one annotator consistently places the wrist a few
  pixels off, so the model learns a mix of conventions and leans towards the
  majority annotator.

### On hit classification

- **Keypoint errors flow into the classifier as noise.** The hit classifier
  relies on the positions of wrists and elbows and on their relations (arm
  angle, wrist height relative to the shoulder, velocity). Small errors on these
  joints distort the features that separate forehand, backhand, smash, volley
  and serve.
- **Timestamp shifts corrupt the temporal targets.** A shifted hit label sits in
  the wrong place relative to the motion that defines it. Frames around the
  contact can become mislabeled negatives or positives, and the timing metric
  becomes unreliable. Fast hits like smashes and volleys are affected most. The
  effect depends on the size of the shift compared with the speed of the hit and
  the length of the temporal window. A few frames of shift inside the window are
  tolerable, while a shift of hundreds of milliseconds is not.

## Detecting keypoint problems at scale

**ViTPose++** is the reference judge. It never saw these labels, so it gives an
independent opinion. It runs offline and only once, so it adds nothing to the
inference cost.

### Step 1 — Gold set

I'd label a small set of a few hundred frames carefully, with several annotators
and a review of the disagreements, so we can assume the keypoints are correct.
The set covers different clubs, camera angles, player scales and annotators, and
includes hard poses (smashes and other overhead hits).

The gold set is split in two: a calibration part and a held-out part used only
for the final validation.

### Step 2 — Bias and noise floor

On the calibration part:

- I run ViTPose++ and measure the **per-keypoint offset** between its
  predictions and the gold labels, per annotator where possible. This captures
  the difference in wrist and elbow convention between ViTPose++ and the
  annotators.
- The **inter-annotator distances** give the noise floor: the natural spread of
  correct labeling.
- The **ViTPose++-to-gold distances**, after bias removal, show how much of a
  measured disagreement is ViTPose++'s own error.

Thresholds come from these distributions. For example: keep anything up to the
90th percentile of the inter-annotator distance, reweight between the 90th and
the 99th, and review anything above the 99th. These numbers are only examples,
the real ones come from the data.

### Step 3 — Scoring the whole dataset

I run ViTPose++ on every annotated frame, and on the clips around the hits (for
the timestamps below). For each annotated keypoint:

- I subtract the offset from Step 2 from the ViTPose++ prediction.
- I normalize the distance to the annotation by **player size** (`d / s`, or OKS
  with a per-keypoint tolerance), so the same pixel error counts the same for
  near and far players.
- I record the ViTPose++ confidence for that keypoint.

### Step 4 — Decision

The confidence decides how much to trust the judge: a large distance only counts
against an annotation when ViTPose++ is confident about that keypoint.

| Condition | Action |
|---|---|
| Distance within the noise floor | **Keep** |
| Slightly above the noise floor | **Reweight**: per-sample or per-keypoint loss weight, decreasing with distance |
| Clearly above, ViTPose++ confident | **Human review**, then fix or drop. The queue is ordered by distance × class importance, so rare classes such as smashes come first |
| ViTPose++ not confident (partial occlusion, blur) | **Keep the frame and mask that keypoint in the loss** |
| Upper body fully occluded, or hit ambiguous | **Filter** |

I keep occluded frames where possible and mask the keypoint instead of dropping
the frame, because these are exactly the cases the model meets in production.
Human review catches the cases where a large distance comes from a ViTPose++
error.

Some noise will always reach the hit classifier in production, so I'd train it
on **predicted or noise-augmented keypoints**. That makes it robust to the
residual noise.

## Timestamp shifts

- The wrist velocity, computed from the ViTPose++ keypoints, peaks close to the
  contact moment. Comparing each annotated hit timestamp against this peak
  flags outliers and reveals per-annotator offsets.
- Tolerances are defined in **milliseconds**, so they mean the same at 25, 30 or
  60 fps (Q1).
- I relabel a shift of hundreds of milliseconds.
- I keep a shift of a few frames. It is absorbed by the temporal window of the
  hit classifier, by label jitter added during training, and by a tolerance
  window in the timing metric.

## Verification

- I validate the flagging on the held-out gold set: precision and recall of the
  flagged keypoints against the known errors.
- I retrain the model on the cleaned and reweighted dataset and evaluate it on
  the held-out gold set, which is excluded from all filtering and reweighting.
- I compare pose metrics (PCK/OKS for wrists and elbows), keypoint jitter and
  per-class hit F1 with timing error before and after.
- After the first cleaning round, I use out-of-fold predictions of the retrained
  model (each sample is predicted by a model that did not train on it) to catch
  residual random noise.
