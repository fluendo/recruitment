# Question 3 — Rare but Critical Smashes

## First check: where are the smashes lost?

I'd use the stage-by-stage test from Q1. If the pose model already gives bad
keypoints on smashes (fast arm, motion blur), that is a separate problem to fix
on the pose side, for example with more smash frames in the pose fine-tuning. If
the keypoints are fine and the hit classifier gives the wrong label, the
strategies below apply. They target the hit classifier, where the class
imbalance matters most.

## Dataset strategies

### Collecting and mining real smashes

Real smashes are the most valuable data, and mining them from unlabeled match
video is cheaper than generating them. I'd pick candidates with the current
model's smash scores. The scores mostly find the smashes the model already
catches, since it is the model that misses the others. So I'd add a simple
keypoint rule (wrist above the head, fast downward arm movement) as a second,
independent way to find candidates. A person reviews the candidates before they
enter the dataset.

### Keypoint-space augmentation of smash clips

I'd augment the trimmed smash clips directly on the keypoint sequences:

- **Structured noise** drawn from the pose model's real error patterns:
  temporal jitter, keypoint dropout under occlusion and systematic offsets.
  Independent Gaussian noise per keypoint is less realistic.
- **Viewpoint changes through 3D lifting**, described in the next section.
- **Time-warping** to vary the swing speed.
- **Left/right mirroring**, with left and right keypoints swapped, where the
  class label does not depend on handedness.

Tradeoffs and risks:

- Perturbations that are too small produce near-copies that add no information
  and lead the model to memorize the original clips.
- Perturbations that are too large produce motions that are no longer smashes.
- I augment **after splitting the dataset**, so that no variant of a validation
  clip appears in training.

### 3D lifting and reprojection from new camera views (main augmentation strategy)

Each real smash clip is lifted to 3D with a monocular 3D pose lifter, and the
3D sequence is reprojected through virtual cameras to produce new 2D keypoint
sequences of the same motion.

- The motion is real, so the smash label stays valid, and the new samples add
  the viewpoint diversity that the training set lacks. This also addresses the
  camera shift from Q1.
- The virtual cameras are sampled within realistic padel setups (height, angle,
  distance, field of view), using the setup metadata logged in Q1.
- The lifter runs once offline, so there is no inference cost.
- Reprojected keypoints are cleaner than real detector output, so I add the
  structured pose-model noise described above afterwards.

Risks and controls:

- Monocular lifting is ambiguous in depth and noisy on fast-moving wrists.
  I check the lifted sequences by reprojecting to the original view, which must
  reproduce the original 2D keypoints, by constant bone lengths, and by temporal
  smoothness.
- Clips that fail these checks are discarded, and I review a sample of the rest
  manually.
- I'd apply the same procedure to the other classes too. If only smashes were
  augmented, the model could learn "looks augmented = smash" as a shortcut.

### Synthetic motion (optional, later step)

I'd only try this later. I estimate 3D body meshes from the existing clips with
a monocular SMPL estimator, a motion generation model creates new smash motions,
and I extract keypoints from them.

- The generated motions must look natural and must still be smashes, so a person
  reviews the output.
- It costs a lot of time and compute, and there is a domain gap to real footage.

## Training strategies

The goal is to make the model pay more attention to smashes during training.
There are three levers.

**1. Give smashes more weight in the loss.** A mistake on a smash costs more
than a mistake on a common hit. Class-balanced sampling (showing smashes more
often in each batch) or focal loss (down-weighting easy examples so training
focuses on the hard ones) achieve a similar effect.

**2. Adjust the decision threshold after training.** The model outputs a smash
score, and a hit counts as a smash above a threshold. Lowering the threshold for
smashes on validation data raises recall without retraining. I'd tune it on
validation and re-check it on new clubs, because the scores can shift with the
camera setup.

**3. Show the model the hardest negatives.** Smashes are mostly confused with
other overhead motions: high volleys and overhead forehands. Training batches
include more examples of these classes, so the model learns the difference
between them and smashes.

Tradeoffs and risks:

- A higher smash weight, or a lower threshold, raises recall and lowers
  precision. The model starts calling high volleys and overhead forehands
  "smash", and the other classes can regress.
- I tune the smash weight and the threshold on validation data, split by match,
  against the product metric: smash recall at a fixed precision, while watching
  the other classes for regressions.

## Evaluation strategy

- The evaluation set keeps the **natural class distribution**, since that is
  what production looks like. A rebalanced set hides false alarms.
- Reported metrics: per-class precision, recall and F1, macro-F1 and the
  confusion matrix, plus a dedicated smash slice.
- The evaluation set contains **only real data**, no augmented or synthetic
  samples, and is split by **match and club**.
- I tune weights and thresholds on a separate validation set, and report the
  final numbers on the evaluation set only once.
- A gain only counts if it is bigger than the seed-to-seed spread measured in Q4
  and holds in each club. I also test each change on its own, to see what
  actually helped.
- Smashes in the evaluation set must be numerous enough for the metrics to mean
  something. Otherwise I use bootstrap confidence intervals and cross-validation
  by match, and collect extra real smashes for evaluation only.
