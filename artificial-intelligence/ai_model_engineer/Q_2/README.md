## Context

The model predicts player keypoints and derives hit events (forehand, backhand, smash, volley, serve) from body and arm motion. The system is already trained and partially deployed, but several performance and reliability issues have appeared in real scenarios.

### Questions:
#### 1. The model shows strong performance on validation data but fails on new matches recorded in different clubs with different camera heights and angles. Hit detection accuracy drops significantly.
##### <br>Explain the most likely root causes of this failure and describe how you would systematically investigate and isolate the problem.

- This is likely a domain shift issue, where the visual model learns to perform well on certain situations but struggles once spatial and contextual information differs too much from the training data.

- One likely culprit is the projected geometry differences introduced by varying camera heights. The model has to learn to predict 3D movements from a 2D projection of the field.The simplest analogy is trying to teach a model to detect a cube in an image: if the training data has only provided cubes from a completely orthogonal perspective, the model would never possibly be able to detect a cube when presented at a 45º angle. During training, it is likely that the data provided did not include enough camera height/angle diversity, causing the system to extrapolate poorly when presented with new 2D projections. In this case, The 2D projection of a padel player hitting a volley varies greatly when changing camera positions. Another possible cause worth looking into is any distinctly differing elements that these issue causing clubs may have, like different turf colors, unusual reflections on the glass elements, or lighting conditions.

- To isolate this issue, we must separate the system into its two components: the pose estimation model and the hit event classification. First, a small ground truth subset of 100 frames of one of the problematic club's footage should be generated by manually annotating the keypoints and hit events. With this small set, we can test each component of the pipeline separately:
  - The pose estimation model's performance can be assessed by running the problematic club's footage sample and extracting Object Keypoint Similarity or Mean Per Joint Position Error metrics with the predicted keypoints vs ground truth. If the error between detected keypoints and ground truth is abnormally high, it would be a sign that the visual domain is too different in this new club from the model's training data and that it is struggling to correctly estimate the poses.
  - Additionally, the hit event algorith must be tested. By comparing the accuracy of the hit detection when ran naively on the 100 frames against the ground truth, we can obtain an estimate of the forms of the classification errors; like detection time shifts/jitter and confusion matrix. Transforming this data in the ways that mimic the change in perspective of a camera height/angle shift and testing the algorithm will be a good way to get a further understanding on the hit event classification limitations.

#### 2. After reviewing some samples, you discover that different annotators labeled keypoints slightly differently (especially wrists and elbows), and hit timestamps are sometimes shifted by a few frames.
##### <br>Analyze how inconsistent annotations affect both pose estimation and hit classification, how you would detect label-quality problems at scale and how you would decide whether to relabel, filter, or reweight parts of the dataset. Justify your decision criteria.

- Incosistent annotation introduces keypoint detection jitter during the pose estimation phase, as the model learns to map these wrists and elbows in a manner that widens the spatial statistical distribution of the keypoints, which shape is dependent on the severity of the errors in annotation in between samples. During the hit classification stage, the error compounds, as the algorithm has to derive extremely precise movements from a noisy distribution of keypoint positions, causing even noisier classification results.

- On the other hand, the timestamp annotation inconsitencies further expands the error of the pose estimation, adding even more jitter to the already jittery keypoint detection. However, this error is most catastrophic on the hit classification step, as detecting a hit derived from the pose requires impeccable accuracy on the timing of the poses themselves, much more so in a sport like padel where the subject is close to the camera and the action is very fast. A difference of just a few frames could mean that the hit is falsely detected during hit preparation, or after the ball has left the racket.

- Detecting misslabeled keypoints at scale could be done by profiling the errors generated by each annotator. The assumption is that if we have determined that certain annotators label wrists and elbows differently, we must have the information on who annotated a certain sample. An implementation would include a very precise pose estimation model, like ViTPose, used to judge the distance between the annotator's labelling and the estimated pose. If the model is confident enough on its estimation, we can safely utilize the euclidean distance as well as the distance jitter between the ground "truth" and the detected to raise a flag for manual review under the suspicion of jittery or displaced labelling. During the review, we should observe what kind of mistakes are present: having totally jittery wrist labels in the training data yields a much worst result than having a wrist labelled 2cm to the right consistently.

- With this same Euclidean distance logic, the temporal frame errors can also be detected. These errors may be corrected by simply comparing the euclidean distance of the ground truth vs estimated keypoints in one frame to the neighboring frames, and determining that the correct frame for that pose is the one with the least distance measured.

- The end goal would be to generate a precise report on each annotator's bias. With this, data generated by certain, very jittery annotators should be filtered out of the training data, as it generates only noise in the final system. In the same manner, the data most consistently annotated should be augmented and/or weighted over other samples.

- If the data available is so little or so bad that losing the worst annotators' data entails losing a significant portion of training data, relabeling could be justified. For this, we should use a pre-trained precise model like the ViTPose. After relabeling, we should study the jitter and accuracy analytics of this new set, as to not repeat the same mistakes. This might be necesary only specifically for certain types of rare hits, like smashes.

#### 3. Smashes are rare in the dataset but very important for the product. The model often misses them while performing well on common hits.
##### <br>Without changing the model architecture, explain which dataset, training, and evaluation strategy changes you would consider. Here, the idea is to discuss tradeoffs and risks of each approach and how you would verify that improvements are real and not overfitting artifacts.

- Smashes are particularly challenging scenarios in padel, as they are probably the fastest type of hit, which makes their detection even more sensitive to noise in the training data.

- Data augmentation techniques can be performed here to synthetically increase the smash data; that is, performing mirroring transformations, color transformations, partial occlusions, perspective shifts, etc. Overfitting is a risk when performing data augmentation on rare classes; as even after augmentation, the data may present certain patterns in body proportions, background and lighting conditions that could cause the model to generalize poorly on smashes in real-life conditions.

- A strategy specifically developed towards compensating rare classes in the data is using the Alpha Balanced Focal Loss. This loss function is able to focus on less present classes by scaling the loss compute based on the predicting confidence; if the model is very confident of a prediction during training, it minimazes this prediction's contribution to the overall loss. Furthermore, the $\alpha$ parameter acts as a class compensating parameter, applying an increased penalty for missing the detection of the smash class during training. This change must go together with data curation and data augmentation, as noisy labels generate an artificially low confidence in the detection, which could aggresively overfit the model to the corrupt data. Two metrics to track whether this loss function is causing the model to overcompensate are the rate of False Positives and the F-1 score.

#### 4. Two training runs with the same configuration produce noticeably different results. One model is stable but less accurate; another is more accurate but produces jittery keypoints and inconsistent hit timing.
##### <br>Provide a reasoning to decide which model is actually better for production. What additional tests, metrics, and scenario-based evaluations would you design to support the decision?

- The first model, less accurate but more stable shall be the preferred solution. The reason behind this decision is that even if less accurate, the added stability during pose estimation avoids the error compounding over to the hit classification stage, as this component is extremely sensitive to jitter.

- The metrics to analyze before choosing either model should be aimed towards detecting each model's jittery behavior, hit timing precision and accuracy:

  - Mean Absolute Acceleration is a metric that can be calculated by analyzing the second derivative of a joint's coordinates through time. This will give us information on how much is the perceived acceleration of the joint varying over time, where a larger MAA will mean higher jitter in pose estimation. This metric aims to test the estimation consistency, not purely accuracy.
  - An F1-Score performed within the neighboring frames of a hit detection event will give us information on the model's temporal accuracy. If one model consistenly detects a hit within a window of 3 frames, it will yield a higher score than one that sometimes classifies it in the exact frame, but other times 5 frames late. This is a good indication on the erraticness of the hit detection.
  - Additionally, tests should be designed to understand the behavior during "stressful" situations for the model, such as video stream frame-dropping, fast paced duels near the net and wall bounce plays where the joints suffer occlusions. The chosen model should be the most robust against the most common problems during production, where it is preferred to have a stabler solution, even if less precise.

#### 5. Which model would you propose for this application? Why?

- As of July 2026, YOLO-Pose-Nano is the state of the art solution for pose estimation. This model utilizes Residual Log-Likelihood Estimation to model the spatial error distribution (closely related to jitter) of each keypoint, effectively increasing its stability for fast sports like padel.
- For Hit Classification, we shall use an option considering the low computational cost desires, like DD-Net. This lightweight model is CNN based, and transforms the Cartesian coordinates to a series of Joint Collection Distances, mapping the distances between joints rather than their pure positions. This would entail that the model would improve the system's robustness to camera angle changes, all while being much faster than other options like Graph Convolutional Network solutions.
