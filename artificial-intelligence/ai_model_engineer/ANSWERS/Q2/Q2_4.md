# Q2.4 — Choosing between stable vs accurate model

Two training runs with the same configuration produce noticeably different results: one is stable but less accurate, and the other is more accurate but produces jittery keypoints and inconsistent hit timing.

**Reasoning for the decision:**

1. **Context and UX:** Since this is a product focused on video analysis (and potentially real-time streaming), temporal stability and visual consistency are far more valuable than a marginal gain in single-frame accuracy. A jittery model produces erratic skeletons and inconsistent hit detections that will severely frustrate the user.

2. **Stability is predictability:** A stable model is predictable — you know exactly what to expect when you deploy it. As an engineering principle, a model that fails consistently is much easier to debug, understand, and improve than a model that succeeds erratically.

3. **"On-paper" accuracy vs reality:** The more accurate model might have inflated metrics due to overfitting to specific, easy static frames. However, if that spatial accuracy comes with temporal jitter, it does not translate into a better product experience. The user perceives the jitter as a bug, not as precision.

4. **Two-stage pipeline argument:** Pose estimation (phase 1) feeds the hit classifier (phase 2). Spatial jitter in the keypoints creates artificial acceleration spikes that will break the hit classifier, leading to a cascade of false positives. A stable pose estimation is more valuable than a precise but unstable one, because the downstream classifier depends on it.

5. **My choice:** I would choose the stable model. In production, consistency and reliability matter more than marginal accuracy gains.

**Additional tests and metrics to support the decision:**

6. **Measure keypoint stability between frames:** Track how much each keypoint (especially wrists and elbows) moves from one frame to the next. In a stable model, keypoints move smoothly. In a jittery model, they jump erratically even when the real movement is fluid. If the implied movement is faster than a human body could physically achieve, the model is unstable.

7. **Check hit timing consistency:** Run the model on the same video multiple times (or on slightly different versions of it, like different video compressions). If the same hit is detected at different timestamps each time, the model is not reliable — a production system should give consistent results.

8. **Test on real match footage:** Validation sets are often clean and curated, not representative of real conditions. Run both models on unedited, continuous match footage and compare the final hit detection F1-score. What matters is the end result of the full pipeline, not isolated accuracy on a clean dataset.
